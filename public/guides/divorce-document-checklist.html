Divorce Document Checklist: What to Gather and How to Organize It

Your attorney bills by the hour. The more complete and organized your paperwork, the less of that time goes to chasing documents.

Divorce involves a lot of paperwork, and most of it is already in your life: tax returns, statements, insurance cards. The challenge is knowing what to find, and getting it into an order someone else can use.

Requirements vary by state and by case, so use this as a starting point for your first meeting with an attorney. Your attorney will tell you what they need.

Financial records

  • Recent tax returns, with W-2s and 1099s.
  • Recent pay stubs, and any records of bonuses or other income.
  • Bank statements for checking and savings accounts, joint and individual.
  • Retirement and investment account statements, such as 401(k), IRA and brokerage accounts.
  • Credit card statements and a list of other debts: loans, car payments, student loans.
  • Mortgage statements, deeds, leases, and recent property tax bills.
  • Records of any business ownership or self-employment income.
  • A list of major assets: vehicles, valuables, and anything bought with significant savings.
  • Records of regular monthly expenses, which help when support is calculated.

Legal records

  • Your marriage certificate.
  • Any prenuptial or postnuptial agreement.
  • Any existing court orders, including restraining orders or earlier custody and support orders.
  • Wills, trusts, and powers of attorney.
  • Any paperwork that has already been filed or served in your case.

Insurance

  • Health, dental and vision cards and plan details.
  • Life insurance policies and named beneficiaries.
  • Home or renters, and auto insurance.

Children's records

  • Birth certificates, Social Security numbers and passports.
  • School records, report cards and any education plans.
  • Medical, dental and vaccination records, and a list of current medications.
  • A list of regular activities, caregivers and childcare costs.
  • A record of your current parenting schedule and any changes, as well as relevant messages between you and your co-parent.

Your own records

  • Your driver's license or ID, Social Security card and passport.
  • A list of your accounts and passwords, kept somewhere only you can see.
  • A timeline of key dates: when you married, separated, moved or changed jobs.

How to organize it

  1. Scan or photograph everything. A clear scan on your phone is fine for most documents.
  2. Use simple folders. Financial, Legal, Insurance, Children, Personal. Name files with a date and a short description, like "2025-checking-statement-march".
  3. Keep the originals safe. Store them in a secure place outside the shared home if possible.
  4. Make a one-page index. A list of what you have and where it is helps your attorney and saves you time.
  5. Add to it as things arrive. New statements and notices keep coming throughout the process.
Mistakes to avoid

Don't access your spouse's private accounts, don't remove original documents, and don't store copies in a place your spouse can open, like a shared computer, shared cloud drive, or family email. If you're unsure whether you can gather something, ask your attorney first.

This guide is general information, not legal advice. What you need to gather depends on your state, your assets, and your situation. Ask your attorney for the exact list.

Common questions

How far back should financial records go?

It depends on your state and your situation, so ask your attorney. Tax returns for several recent years are commonly requested, along with current statements for accounts and debts. Bring more rather than less to the first meeting and let your attorney tell you what matters.

Can I take documents from our shared home or computer?

Gather copies of records you have legitimate access to, such as shared accounts and papers kept at home. Don't log into your spouse's private accounts or devices, and don't remove originals. Doing so can hurt you legally. Ask your attorney before collecting anything you're unsure about.

Should I store these documents on a shared account or computer?

No. Keep copies somewhere only you can access, such as a new email account and a private cloud folder or secure app, with a strong password your spouse doesn't know.
