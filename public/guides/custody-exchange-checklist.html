Custody Exchanges: A Checklist for Calm, Uneventful Handoffs

The handoff is where most co-parenting conflict actually happens: two tired adults, a few minutes, and a child watching. A boring exchange is a successful one.

Whether you share an easy relationship with your co-parent or a strained one, the exchange itself deserves a little planning. The goal is simple: the child moves from one home to the other with their belongings, on time, without a scene, and everyone knows what happens next.

Before the exchange

Most problems at the door are really problems that were left unsettled the day before. A two-minute check removes most of them.

  • Confirm the time and place in writing. A short text or message through your co-parenting app is enough: "Pickup tomorrow at 5:30 at the school lot, as usual."
  • Know who is driving. Many plans say which parent handles transport. If yours doesn't, agree on it so you aren't deciding at the curb.
  • Pack the non-negotiables. Medications, glasses, retainers, a comfort item, homework, uniforms or sports gear, and any device chargers.
  • Pass along important information early. A fever, a test tomorrow, a new prescription. Send it in writing before the handoff, not during it.
  • Decide what stays and what travels. Items that constantly go missing cause constant arguments. Duplicates at each home help.

During the exchange

Treat the exchange like a shift change at work: brief, polite, and about the task.

  • Keep it under a few minutes. The handoff is not the time to discuss money, the schedule, or anything that happened between the two of you.
  • Stay neutral in tone and expression. Children read faces. A calm "Hi, thanks, see you Sunday" does more for them than a perfect argument.
  • Save disagreements for writing. If something comes up, a short "I'll message you about that tonight" ends the conversation without conceding anything.
  • Let the child lead the goodbye. Some kids need a long hug, others want to get in the car. Don't make the moment about your own feelings.
  • Don't send messages through the child. Anything that needs to be said to the other parent should be said to the other parent.
A script for tense moments

"I want to keep this easy for the kids. I'll send you a message about that this evening." Say it once, calmly, and leave.

When your co-parent is late

Lateness is the most common exchange complaint, and the one most likely to turn into a pattern. A steady response helps:

  1. Check your order for a grace period or a rule on lateness.
  2. Send one calm message: "We're at the lot. Are you close?"
  3. Note the scheduled time and the time they actually arrived.
  4. Don't comment on it in front of the child. If it keeps happening, raise it in writing and propose a fix, such as a clear grace period or a different location.

If you ever believe a handoff would put your child at risk, such as the other parent appearing impaired, put safety first and get advice from your attorney about how to proceed. Meridian can't replace that advice.

After the exchange

  • Jot down anything out of the ordinary: late arrival, change of plans, injuries, or anything said that matters for the child's care.
  • Thank the other parent for anything done well. It sets the tone for next time.
  • Send a quick note about the child's day or any needs, if that is part of how you communicate.

This guide is general information, not legal advice. Your custody order controls your exchanges. Where it conflicts with anything here, follow your order and ask your attorney about changes.

Common questions

How long do I have to wait if my co-parent is late for an exchange?

Many orders and parenting plans set a grace period, often 15 to 30 minutes, but yours may say something different or nothing at all. Check your order. If it is silent, agree on a waiting period with your co-parent in writing so lateness doesn't become a recurring argument.

Where is the best place to do a custody exchange?

Schools and daycares are ideal because the child simply goes from one place to another. Otherwise choose a neutral, public, well-lit location that is roughly halfway between homes, such as a library or a busy parking lot. Avoid doing handoffs at either parent's front door if the exchanges have been tense.

What should I write down after an exchange?

Keep it factual: the date, the scheduled time, the actual arrival time, who was present, and anything that affected the child (a missed medication, an injury, or a change of plans). Leave out opinions and adjectives. Dull, accurate notes are the most useful.
