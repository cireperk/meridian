What to Include in a Parenting Plan: A Practical Checklist

Vague parenting plans create conflict. The most useful ones answer the question before anyone has to ask it.

A parenting plan (sometimes called a custody agreement or parenting time schedule) is the document that sets out how you and your co-parent will raise your children apart. Courts in many places require one, and even when they don't, a clear plan prevents most of the avoidable arguments.

The headings below are common in parenting plans. Your state or court may require specific sections and has its own forms, so treat this as a checklist to compare against your own document, not a template.

1. The regular schedule

  • Which days and overnights each parent has during a typical week or two-week cycle.
  • The exact start and end times, including school pickups and drop-offs.
  • A start date for the pattern, so there's no doubt where in the cycle you are.

2. Holidays, school breaks and special days

  • Which holidays matter to your family and how they are shared.
  • How summer, winter break and spring break are divided.
  • Birthdays (the child's and each parent's), Mother's Day and Father's Day.
  • Which schedule wins when a holiday and the regular schedule overlap.

We cover the common ways to split holidays in our guide to holiday custody schedule examples.

3. Decision-making

  • Who decides on education, medical care, religion and major activities, or whether you decide together.
  • What counts as a major decision versus an everyday one.
  • How you resolve disagreements: discussion first, then mediation, before returning to court.

4. Exchanges and transportation

  • Where exchanges take place and who drives.
  • What happens when someone is late, and how long the other parent waits.
  • Rules for car seats and who else may transport the child.

Our custody exchange checklist covers how to keep these handoffs calm.

5. Communication

  • How parents communicate with each other (a co-parenting app, email or text) and how quickly to respond.
  • How the child contacts the other parent during your time: calls, video chat, and when.
  • What information each parent must share: school notices, medical appointments, activities.

6. Expenses and support

  • Who pays for health insurance and how uncovered medical costs are split.
  • Who pays for school fees, childcare, activities and equipment, and in what proportion.
  • How reimbursements are requested and how long the other parent has to pay.
  • Who claims the child for tax purposes.

7. Travel and relocation

  • How much notice is needed for vacations, and whether an itinerary is shared.
  • Rules for out-of-state and international travel, including who holds the passport.
  • What happens if a parent wants to move, including notice periods and distance thresholds.

8. Other people and the child's needs

  • Right of first refusal: whether the other parent is offered time before a babysitter is used.
  • Any agreements about introducing new partners.
  • Provisions for a child's specific needs, such as therapy, special education or a medical condition.

9. A plan for changing the plan

  • How temporary swaps are requested and confirmed (in writing, by a set time).
  • When the plan will be reviewed as the child ages, for example at school transitions.
  • How permanent changes are formalized.
Check for the gaps

Read your plan and ask, for every section, "If we disagreed about this tomorrow, would the document settle it?" Each "no" is a likely future argument. Raise those gaps with your attorney or mediator.

This guide is general information, not legal advice. Requirements for parenting plans vary by state and court. Have a family-law attorney review any plan before you sign it.

Common questions

Do I need a lawyer to write a parenting plan?

Requirements differ by state and by court. Many courts publish forms or templates, and some parents agree on a plan through mediation. Because the plan becomes a binding order once approved, it is wise to have a family-law attorney review it before you sign.

How detailed should a parenting plan be?

Detailed enough that you wouldn't need to ask the other parent what it means. Specific times, locations, and who does what are far easier to follow than phrases like "reasonable visitation." Overly rigid plans can also be a problem, so build in a way to handle changes.

Can a parenting plan be changed later?

Generally yes, but changes usually need to be agreed in writing and, in many places, approved by the court to be enforceable. Plans for toddlers rarely suit teenagers, so many families build in a scheduled review.
