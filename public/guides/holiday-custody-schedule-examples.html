Holiday Custody Schedule Examples: 5 Ways to Split the Holidays

Holidays are where otherwise workable schedules break down. The difference between a calm holiday and a fight is usually one unclear sentence.

A regular weekly schedule is easy to repeat. Holidays aren't, because they happen once a year, they matter emotionally, and they rarely line up with whose turn it is. The way you split them says a lot about how smoothly the year will go.

Here are five common structures. Many plans combine them: a different method for each holiday.

1. Alternating years

One parent has a given holiday in even years, the other in odd years, and it flips the next year.

Example
Mom has Thanksgiving in even-numbered years and Dad has it in odd-numbered years. The other parent has Christmas Eve and Christmas Day in the opposite pattern.

Good for: holidays that can't be shared, or when travel makes splitting impractical. Trade-off: a parent misses a favorite holiday every other year.

2. Splitting the day

The holiday is divided into two parts, and the parents swap which half they have each year.

Example
Christmas morning until 1 p.m. with one parent, then 1 p.m. until the next morning with the other. The next year, they swap.

Good for: parents who live close together. Trade-off: more driving and more handoffs on a day that is already busy for children.

3. Fixed holidays

Each parent always has particular holidays, usually the ones that matter most to their family.

Example
One parent always has the child on the Fourth of July and the other parent's religious holiday, while the other always has Mother's Day or Father's Day.

Good for: families with different traditions. Trade-off: uneven if one parent's holidays are bigger than the other's. Weigh them honestly.

4. Splitting a long break

Winter, spring and summer breaks are divided into blocks of time rather than by day.

Example
Winter break is split at the midpoint. One parent has the first half, including Christmas Eve and Christmas Day, in even years, and the second half, including New Year's, in odd years.

Good for: longer breaks and parents who live far apart. Trade-off: you need an exact definition of when the break starts and ends. See below.

5. Rotating through a fixed list

Holidays are listed, and the parents take turns choosing, or the list rotates over several years.

Example
The plan lists Thanksgiving, Christmas, Easter, Memorial Day and Labor Day. Parents alternate them in order, and the cycle repeats.

Good for: plans with many holidays to cover. Trade-off: more complicated, so write it as a table.

The wording that prevents disputes

Whichever structure you use, most holiday arguments come from details left open. Make sure your plan states:

  • Exact start and end times. "Thanksgiving" should mean something like "Wednesday at 6 p.m. until Friday at 6 p.m.", not just the Thursday.
  • Which year counts. For winter break, say whether it's the year the break begins or the year it ends.
  • Who handles transport. And where the exchange happens.
  • Priority over the regular schedule. The holiday schedule should say it overrides.
  • School closures and long weekends. Say how a Monday holiday or a teacher-training day is handled.
  • What happens if travel is planned. Notice, itineraries, and whether you can take the child out of town.
Put the year on your calendar now

Look up your holidays for the whole year and add them to your calendar today. Knowing in January whose Thanksgiving it is removes a lot of November stress, and makes it easier to plan travel and tell your family.

This guide is general information, not legal advice. Your custody order controls your holiday schedule. If it is unclear, ask your attorney before the holiday, not on the day.

Common questions

Does the holiday schedule override the regular custody schedule?

In most plans, yes: the holiday schedule takes priority during the holiday, then the regular schedule resumes. But this should be stated explicitly in your order. If it isn't, ask your attorney or agree on it in writing before the first holiday.

What does "even years" and "odd years" mean in a custody order?

It refers to the calendar year in which the holiday falls. For example, if one parent has Thanksgiving in even years, they have it in 2026 and 2028, and the other parent has it in 2025 and 2027. Winter break can be trickier because it spans two calendar years, so a good order says which year counts.

What if my co-parent and I want to swap a holiday?

You can, and many families do. Confirm the swap in writing, including exact times and who handles transport. Until a change is formalized, your order is still what applies, so keep a record of what you agreed.
